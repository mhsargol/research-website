Reconfigurable & High-Performance Computing

Origin of the PhD Research

During my computer-architecture studies, I encountered many advanced processor architectures and techniques, including superscalar, VLIW, out-of-order execution, FPGA architectures, and many other advanced approaches. Rather than focusing on one architecture in isolation, I studied their characteristics and limitations.

One observation gradually emerged. Many advanced processor architectures attempted to improve the conventional linear pipeline by making that architecture increasingly sophisticated and "richer."

I started thinking about a different approach. Instead of making the linear pipeline increasingly complex, I considered: What if the pipeline itself became two-dimensional?

The purpose was to reduce the effect of control hazards and increase the effective size of the basic block. At the time, I was thinking about increasing the effective basic-block size from approximately 3–5 instructions to something roughly 2–3 times larger.

I then performed a literature review. During that review, I discovered that an architecture with characteristics related to this idea had already been introduced: Coarse-Grain Reconfigurable Architecture (CGRA).

Building the Research Platform

During the PhD, I discovered that the established tools and libraries that would normally be useful for studying the architecture I wanted to investigate were not accessible or appropriate for my particular CGRA research. Therefore I had to build much of the research infrastructure myself.

I:

  • Implemented the CGRA platform from scratch
  • Wrote C programs that generated transistors and wires for different CGRA modules
  • Used implementation papers and reported electrical/architectural models as the foundation
  • Completed the main architecture
  • Tested its processing capability
  • Developed another C-based simulation/modeling tool for processing delay and energy consumption
  • Used this in-house environment for the University of Tehran laboratory
  • Developed and applied my own optimization algorithm
  • Extracted relevant characteristics
  • Reported the resulting research in publications

The final implementation contained approximately 1.8 million transistors.

CGRA Optimization: "Preserve the Information, Change Its Presentation"

A conceptual idea that I want preserved from the CGRA work is: preserve the information but change its presentation.

This is important because it also helps explain the conceptual relationship I see between the CGRA work and my newer neural-network inference research. The idea is not that every later project uses the same algorithm.

Rather, in some complex systems, the information itself may not need to be changed. A different representation, organization, or presentation can allow the surrounding system to operate much more efficiently.

This is a conceptual connection, not yet a claim that the two projects are technically identical.
